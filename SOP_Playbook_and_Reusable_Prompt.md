# Your Reusable SOP / Motivation Letter Playbook

This is the system we actually used together for Masaryk University and the Czech Government Scholarship, written up so you can reuse it for *any* future scholarship or programme — with any AI assistant, not just me. It has three parts:

1. **The principles** — what actually made those two documents work, so you can judge future drafts (mine or another AI's) against something real.
2. **The Master Prompt** — paste this into a new chat to kick off the process.
3. **Your Personal Profile Sheet** — your reusable "database" of facts, pre-filled from what you've told me. Paste this in right after the Master Prompt so the AI never starts from zero.

---

## Part 1 — The Principles Behind What Worked

**1. Research the institution before writing a word.** The single strongest move in your Masaryk SOP wasn't a sentence about you — it was finding RationAI, understanding what it actually does (2D digital pathology), and noticing what it does *not* yet do (volumetric radiology, low-resource clinical settings). A generic "I admire your excellent faculty" sentence is worth nothing. A specific, checkable gap is worth a page.

**2. One real gap, not five vague ones.** Resist the urge to list every possible reason the institution is great. Pick the one connection that is most specific to your actual work, and spend your limited space building that one argument well.

**3. Evidence over adjectives.** Never write "I am a dedicated and hardworking researcher." Instead: name the thesis, the dataset, the exact percentage figure, the exact correction you made. A reviewer trusts numbers and named artifacts; they discount self-praise automatically.

**4. Disclosed limitations are a strength, not a weakness.** Your thesis's self-correction (retracting an overstated significance test) became one of the more persuasive lines in your SOP, because it signals scientific honesty — a rarer and more valuable trait than confidence. If you have a similar moment of catching your own mistake, use it.

**5. Never let the AI invent a name, statistic, or claim you can't verify.** We only named Prof. Brázdil after I actually searched and confirmed his role and research focus. Any AI (including me) will sometimes produce a plausible-sounding but wrong name, deadline, or fact under pressure to sound specific — every such detail must be indepedently checked by you before submission.

**6. Match structure to the actual constraint, not a generic template.**
   - If given a **strict page limit** (e.g., "one A4 page"): five short paragraphs, no headers eating space, tight sentences, cut ruthlessly.
   - If given a **word/character limit**: count words, don't estimate.
   - If given a **fixed section outline** (like the 5-part structure some scholarships specify): follow their headers/order exactly rather than imposing your own structure — reviewers scan for the sections they expect.
   - If given **no constraint** (a university SOP with no stated limit): 500–800 words is a safe default; longer only if the programme signals they want depth (e.g., PhD/research-heavy programmes).

**7. Personal hardship: brief, factual, dignified — never performed.** One or two sentences stating what happened and what it taught you, then move forward. Long emotional appeals read as manipulative to experienced reviewers; short, controlled ones read as mature.

**8. Always close with a two-way exchange, not just gratitude.** "Thank you for considering me" is weak alone. "Here is specifically what I will bring, and here is specifically how I will use it when I return" is what actually persuades a *development* scholarship committee, who are funding your return, not just your study.

## Part 2 — The Master Prompt

Copy everything in the box below into a new chat with any capable AI assistant (Claude, or otherwise) whenever you start a new scholarship or programme application.

```
You are going to help me write a Statement of Purpose or Motivation Letter for a
scholarship/university application. Follow this process exactly — do not skip
ahead to drafting until you have done steps 1–3.

STEP 1 — Gather the constraints. Ask me, one question at a time (not as a list),
for the following, and wait for my answer before asking the next:
  a) What is the exact document type required (SOP / Motivation Letter / Personal
     Statement) and what institution/scholarship is it for?
  b) What is the exact length constraint (page count, word count, character
     count) and page size (A4/Letter) if specified?
  c) Does the funder/institution require a fixed section structure or set of
     questions to answer? If so, ask me to paste their exact wording, and follow
     their structure and order exactly rather than imposing your own.
  d) What is the specific programme/specialization/department I'm applying to?
  e) What is the submission deadline, so you know how much time we realistically
     have to iterate?

STEP 2 — Research before writing. Using web search, investigate the specific
institution, department, or research group tied to my target programme. Look
specifically for:
  - A specific research group, lab, or professor whose published work is close
    to my own background (I will give you my background in the next step).
  - A genuine, specific gap between what they currently publish/do and what I
    could contribute — not a generic compliment.
  - Any named person or claim you plan to use in the letter: verify it via
    search before using it, and tell me explicitly which claims you verified
    versus which you could not confirm and want me to check myself.
Do not proceed to drafting until you've told me what gap or connection you found
and I've confirmed it's a fair one to use.

STEP 3 — Take my background. I will paste a Personal Profile Sheet (a
standing document of my academic history, work experience, research, personal
context, and goals). Read all of it before drafting. If something relevant to
this specific application is missing from it, ask me for it directly rather
than inventing or generalizing.

STEP 4 — Draft. Write the full document honestly and specifically:
  - Every claim about my work must match what I actually told you — do not
    upgrade "contributed to" into "led," or a single-run result into a proven
    one, or a limitation into a strength, unless I've described it that way.
  - Use real numbers and named artifacts (thesis titles, datasets, percentages)
    instead of adjectives.
  - Keep personal/hardship content to 2–3 sentences maximum: state it, state
    what it taught me, move on.
  - Match the exact structural and length constraint from Step 1 — if it's one
    page, tell me your word count and trim before you show me the draft, don't
    show me something you know is too long.
  - End with a concrete two-way contribution, not just gratitude.

STEP 5 — Flag risk, don't hide it. After the draft, give me a short list of:
  - Any fact, name, or figure I should double-check myself before submitting.
  - Any place where you made an editorial judgment call about tone or framing
    that I might want to revise.

STEP 6 — Iterate. Ask me what to adjust rather than assuming the first draft is
final. If I give you a page/word limit and the draft doesn't fit, recut it
yourself and confirm the new length before presenting it again.

Wait for my first answer to Step 1(a) now.
```

### A closing note on how to use this well

The Master Prompt works whether you feed it to me or to a different AI — it's designed to force the *process* (constraints → research → your real facts → honest draft → flagged risks), not to depend on me personally remembering our conversation. But if you do come back to me in a session where I already have memory of our past conversations enabled, you can skip pasting the Profile Sheet and just say "use what you know about me" — otherwise, always paste it fresh, since a new AI session (or a new chat with me) starts with no memory of today unless you enable and use that feature.
